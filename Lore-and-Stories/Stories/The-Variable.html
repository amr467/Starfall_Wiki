The Variable

The Variable

The following account was reconstructed from two sources: a partial personal journal recovered from the outer archive’s miscellaneous correspondence division, and an internal Vigil deliberation record recovered separately, by means not established. Neither source references the other. They have been arranged here because, placed together, they describe the same event from two directions.

The journal’s author is identified in the outer archive only by a district posting code. The deliberation record refers to the same individual by a risk designation. Neither source uses a name.

— The Archivist

I.

Beth had been in Border District Fourteen for eleven years, and in that time she had learned to process what she saw the way she processed supply manifests — methodically, without attaching to the details, moving on.

The advance had reached the valley three weeks ago. She had watched it from the depot roof because the depot faced east and there was nowhere else to look. The constructs came over the ridge in the early morning, and they kept coming, and the valley — which she had seen on survey maps as three settlements, marked in the standard notation, medium density, agricultural — was by afternoon something the maps would re-annotate as a transit corridor. She had processed that paperwork herself. Four minutes. She was good at her job.

What the paperwork did not contain was the smell that reached the depot on the second day. Nobody discussed it. She didn’t discuss it either. She went home and wrote about it in her journal, because writing things down had always been how she confirmed to herself that something had actually happened. She was not planning anything. She understood that clearly. She had watched from a roof and smelled what was left behind and written four pages, and that was the whole of it.

She had lived alongside the advance for years without writing anything down. The notation system was not ambiguous if you could read it, and she had been reading it for nine years. She had made a quiet decision early on — she could not have said exactly when — not to look too closely at what the numbers described. It was not cowardice, or not only that. It was the understanding that she had built something here, a life that worked, and the life required a particular kind of attention that excluded certain questions.

She had read V. Sorath’s treatise when it circulated. Most of the administrative class had. She had found it persuasive in the way arguments are persuasive when they confirm something you have already decided. The line that stayed with her was the one about the mandate not being chosen by individuals, only inherited by peoples. She had found it comforting at the time. She was less sure about it now, which was why she had written four pages instead of none.

She kept the journal under a false bottom in her desk drawer. She had built it herself, about eighteen months ago, for no particular reason she could name at the time. It had seemed prudent.

She replaced the false bottom and went to make dinner. Outside, the constructs moved along what the maps now called a transit corridor, and the evening settled over the district the way evenings always had, indifferent to what the afternoon had been.

[Internal record. Permanent Witness Body, deliberation files, ~3,500 BCE. Recovered separately from the outer archive. Classification at time of filing: body records only.]

RISK ASSESSMENT — DESIGNATION: DEPOT-14-SOUTH-LOGISTICS-F

Convened: full inner body. Matter: passive observation, frontier district. Status: under assessment.

Summary of matter:

Subject is a logistics administrator, Border District Fourteen, nine years in post. No prior flags. Performance assessments consistently satisfactory. No documented contacts of interest.

Subject was observed on the depot roof during the valley clearance operation, approximately dawn to mid-morning, Day One of the clearance. Subject’s position gave unobstructed sightlines to the eastern ridge approach. Duration of observation: estimated three hours.

Subject has not filed any report referencing the clearance. Subject has not discussed the clearance with any monitored contact. Subject has made no documented attempt to access restricted survey records beyond her standard remit.

Subject’s personal quarters were assessed on Day Four. A journal was located beneath a false panel in a desk drawer. Contents are attached. The journal predates the valley clearance by approximately two years and contains routine personal material. The four most recent entries reference the clearance directly. They are observational in character. They contain no plans, contacts, or intent to report. They contain accurate descriptions of what the subject observed, and a record of the subject’s decision not to report it.

The false panel is of note. It was constructed by the subject and predates the clearance by at least eighteen months, suggesting habitual concealment practice rather than a response to this specific event.

Body deliberation:

First position: The subject has done nothing actionable. Nine years of satisfactory service. No contacts of interest. No report filed. The journal contains no actionable content. The appropriate response is continued monitoring.

Second position: The subject witnessed a clearance operation at close range, documented her observations accurately, and made a deliberate decision to conceal those observations. The concealment was not reactive — the false panel predates the event by eighteen months. We do not know what she concealed before the valley clearance. We know only what we found.

Third position: The subject’s journal records a decision not to report. Decisions can be revisited. She is stable now. She may not be stable in three years, or ten. She may encounter someone worth speaking to. The entry that concerns this body most is not the four pages about the clearance. It is the single line referencing the Sorath treatise: I have made a decision not to examine the foundations. A person who has decided not to examine the foundations has acknowledged the foundations exist. That acknowledgment does not expire.

Response to third position: Acknowledged.

On monitoring as sufficient: Monitoring produces information. It does not produce certainty. The body’s purpose is the production of certainty. The subject’s risk profile will not change in a direction that reduces risk. She has seen what she has seen. She has written what she has written. Time does not remove these facts.

On the subject’s service record: Noted and set aside. The subject’s service record is not under assessment.

On proportionality: The body does not assess proportionality. The body assesses risk. The question is not whether the subject deserves what follows. The question is whether the variable can be left open. The body’s unanimous position is that it cannot.

Decision: closure authorized. Designation passed to field operations. Standard protocol. No further documentation will be issued on this matter.

II.

Three days later, Beth’s supervisor noted her absence at morning roll.

The depot log had her signing out the previous evening at the standard time. Her quarters showed no disturbance. Her desk was in order. The false panel in the bottom drawer was open and empty, which he noticed and did not know what to make of and did not record.

He filed a missing persons report. The Office of Administrative Oversight responded within the day: the subject had been reassigned to another posting, effective immediately, transfer order mislaid in transit, apologies for the confusion.

He accepted this. He closed the desk drawer. He did not mention the false panel in any subsequent document, because mentioning it would have required explaining how he knew it was there, and explaining that would have required him to say that he had gone through her desk, and going through her desk would require a reason, and he did not have a reason except that something felt wrong and he had been in Border District Fourteen long enough to know that feeling wrong and saying so were two different things with two different outcomes.

He had been here for six years. He was good at his job.

The journal was not in the drawer. The body’s field record notes, in its final line: personal effects secured and destroyed per standard protocol.

What the field record does not note — because the body already knew, and the body’s records are for the body — is that three members of the field operations team had been in Beth’s quarters before she came home that evening. They had opened the desk. They had read the four pages. They had replaced the false bottom and left, and she had come home and made dinner and gone to bed and the variable had been open for one more night before it was closed.

She had decided not to examine the foundations. She had been careful. She had built a false panel and kept her head down and done her job for nine years and it was not enough, and it was never going to be enough, because the body was not asking whether she had done anything. The body was asking what she might do. The answer, as far as the body was concerned, was the same for everyone who had seen what she saw and written what she wrote.

I have compiled worse things than this. I am not certain why this one is harder to set down.

— The Archivist

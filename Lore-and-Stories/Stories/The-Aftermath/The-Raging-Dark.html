Chapter 4: The Raging Dark

Part 1 — The Dispatch / The Warning

MOLTEN VIGIL — INTERNAL DISPATCH
Classification: Obsidian Tier — Operative Eyes Only
Recipient: Field Agent V. — Designation: Shadow Walker
Re: Convergence Event — Settlement Designation: Hunter’s Rest

The Raging Variant has been confirmed in proximity to the settlement at Hunter’s Rest. Estimated convergence: one cycle of daylight. This timeline is not approximate. Do not treat it as approximate.

The anomaly is present within the settlement. Its continued survival is the priority. The Variant’s approach places that survival at unacceptable risk.

You are tasked with the following, in order of precedence: ensure the Variant is neutralized. Ensure the settlement is preserved. Ensure the anomaly remains alive — at any cost, including exposure. If neutralizing the Variant requires revealing your presence and your affiliation, you are authorized to do so. The anomaly must not be lost. Failure on this point is not a contingency we are prepared to absorb.

The anomaly is not to be engaged, detained, or made aware of its significance. Observe. Protect. Report.

This dispatch supersedes all prior operational parameters.

— ⊘

They came back to the tavern the way things come back from hard places — slowly, and without ceremony.

Figo walked. That was the most generous description available. He moved forward with the grinding determination of a man who had decided that falling down was not an option and was making his body agree through sheer force of will. Sheela was back on his shoulder but the arm below the wound hung slightly wrong, and every step on uneven ground produced a tightness at the corner of his jaw that he would never acknowledge and no one would mention.

Trista had gone somewhere past tired into a state that didn’t have a name — the particular hollowness of a healer who has given until the well is dry and then given a little more on top. Her eyes were open. They were pointed forward. Whether they were seeing anything was a separate question. She put one foot in front of the other with the mechanical patience of someone running on a very old, very stubborn instinct.

James was, by every observable metric, completely fine. He walked at the same unhurried pace he always walked, hands in his pockets, occasionally humming something tuneless under his breath. If the night in the forest had cost him anything, he had hidden the receipt.

Vael had not slept. This was apparent in the way it is always apparent in people who believe they are hiding it — the slight delay between stimulus and response, the eyes that moved a half-second behind everything else, the hands that kept finding nothing to do and didn’t know where to put themselves. He had built the fire, kept the watch, listened to the treeline, and spent the hours until dawn turning over a name that felt like his own and a shadow that had come from somewhere he couldn’t see.

The tavern’s warmth hit them as Ragnik’s door swung open — woodsmoke and the smell of whatever had been on the fire since yesterday, and underneath it the particular human density of too many people in not enough space.

Hunter’s Rest had always been full since the Starfall. Now it was full in a way that pressed against the walls. Every bench was occupied. Every spare foot of floor near the hearth had someone on it — rolled in cloaks, folded against their packs, children tucked against parents in the instinctive geometry of people trying to take up less space. A man near the far wall sat with his elbows on his knees and a cup between his hands and stared at nothing with the specific blankness of someone who had run out of things to think. Two women at a corner table held each other’s hands across the wood and didn’t speak. Near the stairs, three children slept in a pile like animals, sharing warmth, their faces young enough to still look peaceful even here.

The cold had crept in through the gaps — the fire fought it, but the fire was one fire and the cold was everywhere.

“Oh!” Rebecca appeared from between two occupied tables, her expression cycling through relief and concern and professional warmth in rapid succession. She took them in — all four of them, the state of them — and did not flinch, because Rebecca had been taking people in and not flinching for weeks. “Look who made it back. How did the mission go? Any surprises, I’d wager?”

“You’d win that wager,” James said, with a smirk that cost him nothing.

Trista sighed. Figo sighed. Vael sighed.

“We need rooms,” Vael said. He heard the exhaustion in his own voice and decided he didn’t have the energy to do anything about it. “Please. Anything available.”

Rebecca’s face did something apologetic. “We’re running low, I won’t lie to you. End of the world and all that.” She spread her hands. “I’m not sure what I can—”

“They’ll have rooms.”

Ragnik came through from the service end of the bar, wiping his hands on a cloth, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man who had already decided. He looked at each of them in turn — the same evaluating look he’d given Vael the morning he’d woken up in room ten, but broader now, taking in all four. He let it settle for a moment.

“You’re the strongest party we’ve got under this roof,” he said. “I’ve been keeping four rooms.” He didn’t explain why. He said it in the tone of a man whose instincts had never owed anyone an explanation. “Something’s coming. I don’t know what yet. But I know I want you four at full capacity when it does.”

James looked at Ragnik. Something shifted in his expression — brief, almost invisible, the faintest acknowledgment of a man who recognized another person operating on information they couldn’t fully articulate. His eyes moved away before it could become a conversation.

Trista, Figo, and Vael all looked at James.

James’s face was alert. Not alarmed — the odds weren’t there for alarm, not yet — but the theatrical ease had settled into something quieter and more watchful. It was a small change. It was enough.

Convenient, Vael thought, watching him, to have someone who can read the shape of what’s coming. Even when he’s goofing around, there’s something underneath it. Something he’s not telling us about the limits of what he can see. Maybe it’s not unlimited. Maybe the information has gaps. He filed it away.

“Right then,” Rebecca said, already moving. “Four rooms. Give me a moment to sort them and I’ll have you set up. Hang in there, the lot of you — you look like you’ve earned a bed and then some.”

They went up one by one as the rooms were readied. Figo made it to his door, went through it, and the sound of something very large settling onto something that creaked significantly came through the wall approximately four seconds later. Trista’s door closed with a soft, final click. Vael stood in his doorway a moment, looking back at the stairs.

James was still down there. He could hear him settling onto a barstool, the familiar sound of a cup being set on wood.

Vael closed his door.

The dream came in fragments, the way it always did — not a story but a sensation, a sequence of images that felt more real than the room around him even as they slipped.

Stars. Not the gentle stars of a clear night but falling ones, a cascade of light tearing down from a sky that had gone wrong, each one trailing fire. And between Vael and the falling sky a figure — white, luminous, arms spread wide, holding something back. Holding it back. Not succeeding, but trying. Still trying.

And a voice.

—YOU MUST—

—DON’T LET IT DESTROY—

—YOUR OUR ONLY HOPE MY CHILD—

—PLEASE REMEMBER ME—

—i lo—

Vael came awake the way you surface from deep water — all at once, gasping, the ceiling of the inn room arriving in his vision like something dropped from a height. His heart was going too fast. He was drenched in cold sweat, the blanket twisted around him, the early grey of pre-dawn sitting in the window.

He lay still until his breathing evened out. Then he raised his hand in front of his face and looked at it.

I need to remember. The thought was steadier than he expected, coming from somewhere below the panic. I can feel it getting closer. Like something is on the other side of a door and I’m almost strong enough to push it open. He lowered his hand. But there’s something missing. A key.

He said it out loud, quietly, to no one. “A key to my memories.”

The knock came a minute later — two soft raps, spaced carefully, light enough that a sleeping person would have slept through them. He recognized the consideration in it before he recognized who it was.

He opened the door.

Trista stood in the corridor, not quite meeting his eyes. She was composed the way people are composed when they’ve been awake long enough to move past tired into something more deliberate — her hands still, her posture straight, all the fraying edges of last night tucked away.

“I know it’s early,” she said, to a point somewhere past his shoulder. “May I come in?”

“Yes. Of course.”

She moved past him into the small room, and he stepped back to give her space. She looked at the window first — the sun was beginning, a thin line of pale gold cresting whatever was left of the treeline, falling across the white snow on the ground below and the dead branches above it. She stood and looked at it for a moment, and he let her.

I’ve never actually talked to her, Vael realized. Not properly. She’s been next to me through all of this and I don’t know anything about her. He watched her look at the window. She said she knew what my power meant. She’s been sitting on that since the Framedious.

“When we fought the Framedious,” she said, still looking at the glass. “You remember what I said.”

“That we’d talk later about my powers.” He moved to sit on the edge of the bed, leaving her the chair by the window. “I didn’t think about the timing — it just happened. Like the Dagaruma. My body knew something before I did.”

“You made a hand sign,” she said. “Right before the wolf came. Do you remember it?”

He did. He raised his hand, and let his fingers find the shape — the same shape they’d found in the road with the Framedious charging and his mind entirely blank. Two knuckles up. The rest folded in. The shadow it cast on the wall between them, even in the thin early light, looked like something crouching.

Nothing happened. No wolf. No darkness rising from the floor.

But they both looked at the shape of it for a long moment.

“The Shadow Wolf,” Trista said quietly.

“Is that what it is?”

She sat down in the chair, and the morning light fell across her face, and she looked out the window for another moment before she began.

“Before I was a hunter,” she said, “I was in a group that worshipped Lumia. I didn’t know it was a cult — I want to be clear about that. I was young, and Lumia herself…” She paused. “Her passion for healing was genuine. She brought people back from the edge. She believed everyone deserved a chance at life, and she lived that belief. I admired her.” Her hands settled in her lap. “But things that grow too large develop problems in their edges. People began using Lumia’s name to extract taxes from villages, to build her statues, to manufacture quarrels with communities that wanted nothing to do with any of it. When the villages finally pushed back, they needed help. A mage came to their aid.”

She looked at him.

“She had the same powers you do.”

The silence that followed was very quiet.

“You are a Dragodor,” Trista said. The morning light was rising across her face as she said it, slow and steady. “Vael Dragodor.”

The word hit him somewhere below thought.

His legs went first. He had time to register that he was falling before the floor rushed up to meet his knees, and then he was on the ground and the room was doing something wrong around the edges, and from somewhere that was not the room — from somewhere behind everything, behind the fog, behind the door he hadn’t been strong enough to open — a voice came through.

Not a fragment this time. Not a partial signal breaking up across distance.

All of it. Finally all of it.

VAEL. YOU MUST DEFEAT THE DRACOL. DON’T LET IT DESTROY WHAT WE HAVE BUILT. YOU ARE OUR ONLY HOPE, MY CHILD. PLEASE REMEMBER ME. MY NAME IS SAELA—

And then, quieter. The quiet that comes at the end of things.

I love you, son.

Trista was beside him before he fully came back to the room, her hands on his shoulders, steadying him into the chair by the window. He let her. His breathing was ragged. The morning light was very bright.

“I’m here for a reason,” he said, when he could say anything. He heard how his own voice sounded — not afraid, exactly. Something more specific than afraid. “I’m the only one who can defeat the Dracol.” He looked at his hands. Both of them. “Can I even do it? Am I really—” His breath caught. “I only summoned it by accident. Once. I don’t even know how I did it.”

Trista’s hand found his and the healing light came through — not dramatic, not the battlefield version, just something warm and steady moving into his chest, slowing the pace of things down to something manageable.

“Listen to me,” she said. “The Shadow Wolf is the first. The Dragodor didn’t have one power — they had three.” She kept her eyes on his. “The shadow is what always follows us. The spirit is our soul. The physical body is what we’re made of.” She let that settle. “Your clan mastered all three. And there is a fourth — the perfected form of all of them combined. I don’t know its full nature. But I know that what you showed yesterday is only the beginning of what you carry.”

Vael looked at his trembling hand. Watched it slow.

“The Raging Dracol could be miles away,” Trista added, with the measured calm of someone offering a true thing. “Likely sleeping. Probably not even near us yet.”

He breathed out. Let himself believe it for a moment. Let the panic settle into something smaller and more workable.

They both heard the screaming from downstairs at the same moment.

They came down the stairs fast, Figo materializing in the corridor behind them — pale from the venom, moving carefully, but moving — and falling into step without a word.

The tavern had changed.

The careful, exhausted stillness of the early morning had broken. People were on their feet, pressed back toward the walls, creating a wide, involuntary radius around what had been left in the center of the room. Ragnik was on his knees beside it, and his face — Ragnik’s face, which Vael had only ever seen weathered and certain and quietly kind — was doing something it clearly had no practice at.

The man on the floor had been a warrior. That much was still visible in the structure of him — the build, the gear, the way even collapsed on the ground his body held a shape that spoke of training. Everything else had been altered by something that had come from outside and remade him.

His skin was wrong. On the left side of his body it had gone the color and texture of cooling coal — cracked, darkened, the deep black-grey of something that had been exposed to a heat beyond what skin was built to survive. The cracks glowed faintly at their edges, dull orange, as though the fire that had done this was still somewhere inside him and had not yet entirely finished. His breathing was audible from across the room — shallow and wet and wrong, each inhale a visible effort, each exhale a sound that made the people nearby flinch and look away.

“He’s under a healing spell,” Trista said, low and quiet, stepping forward to look. “Strong one — not strong enough to fix this, but enough to hold him together. Enough to let him move. Enough to let him come here.” She looked at Vael. “Someone sent him.”

Ragnik looked up. “Rebecca,” he said, quietly. “Water. A bowl. A towel.”

Rebecca was already moving.

Vael looked for James.

He was at the back of the gathered crowd — not in the center where the drama was, not pressing forward with the rest of them. Standing back, slightly apart, very still. His hands were at his sides. His face—

Vael had catalogued James’s faces in the short time he’d known him: the theatrical ease, the smug satisfaction, the performed innocence, the rare flicker of genuine sincerity beneath all of it. He had not seen this one. He had not known this one existed.

James was looking at the man on the floor and the probabilities were arriving in him and they were arriving badly. His eyes moved across the room — across every face, every person, the children by the fire, the women at the corner table — with the particular horror of someone watching a number they cannot change get closer to zero.

It is truly a curse, Vael thought. And for the first time he meant it without reservation.

The man on the floor drew one more breath. His eyes — one of them undamaged, the other filmed with white — found Ragnik’s face and held it.

“En…raged.” The word came out in pieces. “Dra…col.” A breath. The orange glow at the cracks in his skin pulsed once, faint. “…Clo…se.”

The bowl arrived. Rebecca set it down with careful hands and a face that had already understood what careful hands were for here.

It was too late.

The room held the silence for one long second.

Then Rebecca set the towel down beside the bowl, very slowly, and her face crumpled in the way that faces crumple when a person is trying very hard not to let them. “He didn’t deserve this,” she said, quietly. Her voice was steady. Her eyes weren’t. “No one does.”

The silence broke all at once.

The tavern erupted — not the clean panic of a single shock but the layered, compounding panic of people who had already been holding their breath for months and had just been told to stop. Voices collided with each other. Someone was crying. Someone was shouting. Near the door, two warriors in mismatched armor were bent over a table, a rough map spread between them, arguing in low urgent voices about approaches and terrain and the size of the beast and whether they had anything that could stop it.

The answer, in their faces, was visible. They knew the answer. They were arguing anyway, because arguing was something to do.

A child near the hearth woke up and looked around at the panicking room and began, quietly and without drama, to cry.

Vael stood in the middle of it and felt the shape of things settling around him — the dying man, the mother’s voice finally finished, the wolf that had come from his shadow, the name Trista had given him like a key turning in a lock — and understood, with the particular clarity that arrives sometimes in the center of chaos, that the door he’d been trying to open was open now.

He just didn’t know yet what was on the other side.

Part 2 — The Plan

The noise in the tavern had reached the kind of pitch where it stopped being individual voices and became a single, shapeless thing — panic compressed into sound, bouncing off the low ceiling and the packed walls until it was everywhere at once.

Vael heard none of it.

He was looking at the dead warrior on the floor, and the world had narrowed to that — the cracked, coal-dark skin, the faint glow that had already begun to fade at the edges of the wounds, the face of a man who had used the last of everything he had to come here and say two words. The sounds of the room reached him muffled, as though through deep water. His own breathing was too loud. His chest was doing something tight and involuntary and he couldn’t make it stop.

Then the footsteps came down the stairs. Heavy, deliberate, the particular rhythm of a large man moving carefully because his body was asking him not to move at all and he was declining the request.

Figo reached the bottom step. He took in the room — the chaos, the noise, the faces — and he drew a breath so deep it seemed to pull the air from the entire floor.

“WHOAAAAA—”

“QUIET!”

The word didn’t echo. It didn’t need to. It landed like a physical thing, filling every corner of the room simultaneously, and the silence that followed was so complete and so sudden that the ears rang with it — a high, thin tone that hung in the air for a full second before fading, as though the sound itself needed a moment to realize it was over.

Every face in the room turned to Figo.

The children had stopped crying. The arguing warriors had stopped arguing. The women at the corner table had gone still. Even the fire seemed to have quieted.

Vael blinked. The water drained. The room came back in full — sharp, loud in its silence, every detail present. He exhaled.

Ragnik moved into the quiet before it could fill back up with something worse. “Children,” he said, in the voice he used when a thing was simply going to happen. “Elders. Anyone who isn’t here to fight.” He gestured toward the stairs. “Upstairs. Now. We need the floor.” He paused. “Pray for us.”

Nobody argued with Ragnik. They filed past Figo on the stairs — one by one, some touching the big man’s arm as they passed, most just moving with their eyes down, carrying their fear quietly because Ragnik had asked them to and this roof had been keeping them alive for weeks. A child looked back from the third step. Figo caught the look and gave a slow, steady nod. The child turned and kept climbing.

When the last of them had gone, what remained in the tavern was around twenty — warriors, mages, healers, hunters of varying capability. A handful who carried themselves like people who had survived hard things. Several who looked like the hard thing they were currently facing was the hardest thing they’d encountered. Two or three who Vael could read from across the room were already calculating the odds and not liking the numbers.

He walked to the table with the map.

Two warriors were bent over it when he arrived — both human, both road-worn in the particular way of people who had been moving since the Starfall and hadn’t found a reason to stop. The taller one had a broadsword across his back and a jaw that looked like it had been broken at least twice and reset at home. The other was stockier, shorter, with the kind of forearms that came from years of work rather than training, a hand axe at each hip. They both looked up when Vael approached and neither of them looked especially encouraged by what they saw.

“We’re doomed,” the taller one said, with the flat certainty of a man delivering a weather report.

“The timing makes no sense,” added a mage standing nearby — a woman in practical grey robes with silver-thread embroidery at the cuffs, a travel-worn staff propped against the table beside her, ink stains on three fingers of her right hand. “No Dracol should be active in winter. The cold alone should keep it dormant.”

“It’s one hundred percent enraged,” said a healer on the other side of the table. She had the clipped precision of someone trained in diagnosis — young, serious, a green sash at her waist marking her guild rank. “The behavioral profile fits. No normal Dracol moves like this.”

“You’re correct,” Trista said, moving up beside Vael. “On all counts. This is not a standard threat profile.”

Vael wasn’t listening to any of them. He was looking at the map.

It was rough — hand-drawn, updated in different inks at different times, the tavern marked at the center with a ring of terrain sketched around it. Forest to the west. Open ground to the south. And to the northeast, where the treeline thickened and the land pinched between two ridgelines—

“I think we have a chance,” he said.

The table went quiet. He felt them looking at him — measuring, some of them skeptical — and kept his eyes on the map.

“Why is it coming here?” he asked. “Specifically here.”

“They seek flesh to burn.” Figo’s voice came from just behind him, low and controlled. Vael could hear the effort the control was costing. “An enraged variant more so. It hunts living things. The concentration of people in this building—” He stopped. Vael didn’t look back at him. He didn’t need to see the shivering to know it was there.

“How many horses does the tavern have?” Vael asked, looking at Ragnik.

Ragnik considered. “Fifteen. Give or take.”

Vael put his finger on the map. “Fifteen riders. We use them to draw the Dracol away from the building — pull its attention, get it moving.” His finger traced northeast. “We lead it here. This chokepoint between the ridgelines.” He looked up. “Ten minutes at a full gallop. Once it’s in the channel, we have two ridgelines full of ranged fighters above it. Arrows, bolts, magic — everything we have, concentrated into a kill zone it can’t easily move through.”

The silence that followed was the silence of people doing rapid, uncomfortable mathematics.

It was broken by a sound — a soft, dry cough — and a figure stepped out from between two of the assembled mages.

He was short. Dramatically so — the kind of short that in another context might have produced jokes, but the way he carried himself discouraged them. He wore robes of deep burgundy with brass buttons at the collar, and a single round lens on a thin brass frame over his left eye that caught the firelight when he moved. His face was calm in the way of someone who had spent a long time thinking about terrible things and had arrived, through effort, at equanimity. He looked to be in his mid-thirties, though the eyes were older.

“It won’t be that simple,” he said. Not unkindly. He walked to the table and looked at the map with the focused attention of someone reading a text. “You’re thinking of it like a predator that can be lured. It is not only a predator.” He looked up at Vael. “Where I come from, they call them world-enders. Not for the scale of their destruction alone — for what they are drawn to.” He tapped the map. “It seeks fear. It senses life and it wants to end it. All of it. Every living thing in this building is a signal it can read.” His eyes were steady. “And it is intelligent. Intelligent enough that a simple bait maneuver will not hold it if it senses the deception.”

The room absorbed this.

“Barrier mages,” Vael said. “Do we have any?”

Four hands went up. One of them belonged to a man who stepped forward without being asked — tall, lean, early thirties, with the slightly distracted quality of someone whose mind was perpetually running calculations. His staff was long and plain, no ornamentation, the wood worn smooth from years of handling. His guild pin was silver, three rings — experienced, not elite, but close.

“How long can the four of you sustain a full barrier?” Vael asked. “Strong enough to mask scent as well as presence?”

The barrier mage looked at his three colleagues. Some silent communication happened. “Twenty-five minutes,” he said. “Full concealment. After that we start degrading.”

“That’s enough,” said a voice from the back of the room. “Probably.”

James had drifted, at some point, to the rear of the gathered crowd. He was leaning against the wall with his arms folded, and the stress that had been written so visibly across his face had faded — not gone, but reduced, the numbers apparently settling into a configuration he could work with. He needed information to see clearly. He was getting it. “Still,” he added, “let’s not cut it closer than we have to.”

“The children, elders, and non-fighters go into the shelter,” Vael said to Ragnik. “The barrier buys us twenty-five minutes. We need to force the Dracol to run — get it moving toward the chokepoint — before that barrier drops.”

“Can we even make it run?” the warrior with the broken jaw asked. “Without ballistae?”

The door opened.

It opened hard — not violently, but with the particular force of someone who has decided that knocking is not a relevant consideration. Cold air moved through the room, and in it, framed for a moment in the grey morning light, was a figure that seemed to bring the dark in with her.

She was tall and still in the way of things that are only still when they choose to be. Her coat was the color of deep shadow, long, fitted, the fabric catching no light at all. Beneath it, glimpsed as she moved, the outline of a bow across her back — long, dark, strung with something that didn’t look quite like conventional string. Her hair was dark, pulled back, and her face was composed with the cold precision of someone who had learned long ago to keep everything internal. Her eyes moved across the room in a single sweep — map, faces, the dead warrior on the floor, Vael — and the sweep took approximately one second and appeared to be complete.

She had the quality of a shadow that had learned to move without a light source.

Vael’s mind went to the treeline. To the space between two trees in the dark. To the shape that had been there and then wasn’t. Same silhouette, he thought. Same density.

“The Dracol’s scent range exceeds that distance,” she said, without preamble, without introduction. She walked to the table. “An enraged variant’s range is higher still. At the pace it’s moving, your barrier window and your riding distance don’t align. It will smell the shelter before your mages reach their limit.” She looked at the map. “You need to force it to commit to the chase before the barrier drops. Which means the bait needs to move sooner, and faster, and be compelling enough that it doesn’t stop to investigate the building.”

The silence in the room was a different kind now — not the silence of panic suspended, but the silence of people listening.

“Is that even possible?” the ink-fingered mage asked, frowning. “Without ballistae to threaten it into flight?”

Vael looked at James.

James was looking at Varesa. The stress had not just reduced — it had evaporated. His face was undergoing a transformation of such visible relief and admiration that it bypassed subtle entirely and arrived directly at theatrical. His hand went to his chest. His eyes had gone slightly glassy.

“No,” James said, to the room at large, without looking away from her. “We have something better.” He straightened, addressed her directly, and performed a bow of such deep and genuine respect that it would have been at home in a formal court. “You’ll help us, won’t you?”

Varesa looked at him. Something shifted in her expression — a slight recalibration, the adjustment of someone who has just correctly identified an unusual variable. She knew what he was. Or knew enough.

“Name’s Varesa,” she said. “And yes — today, the odds will be on your favor.”

She almost smiled when she said it. Almost.

James straightened from his bow and placed one hand over his heart. The other found his coat pocket, produced the rum flask, looked at it, put it back. He appeared to be experiencing an emotion he didn’t have a prepared response for. “I—” he began.

“James,” Trista said.

“I am fine,” he said, with great dignity, fanning himself slightly.

Vael turned back to the map before his face did something inappropriate.

“Two routes to the chokepoint,” he said, tracing both with his finger. “The short path — three minutes at speed. The long path — five minutes. If we split our riders—”

“How do we ensure it takes the long path?” Varesa asked.

“We put ten riders on it,” Vael said. “More life, more fear, stronger signal. The Dracol will follow the larger group.” He looked up. “Our five strongest long-range fighters take the short path, reach the ridge first, and set the ambush. The ten draw it in.”

“That’s insane.” One of the warriors — younger, a scout’s build, a shortbow across his back — shook his head. “If the ten riders can’t stay ahead of it—”

“We take our chances,” James said. The theatrical warmth had settled back into something quieter and more certain. “It’s the highest probability path we have. I’d stake something on it.” He paused. “I am, in fact, staking something on it.”

“It’s still not enough,” Varesa said.

She moved around the table toward Vael, and the room subtly reorganized itself to give her passage — not dramatically, just the small, unconscious adjustments people make around someone whose presence takes up more space than their physical dimensions account for.

“The shelter will mask your non-combatants,” she said, “but the Dracol will still sense the weight of life in this building if it passes close enough. It may split its attention.” She looked at the map. Then at Vael. “We take half the non-fighters on the long path. In carts. More life, more signal. It commits fully to the chase and doesn’t double back.”

The room erupted.

“Absolutely not—”

“That’s the people we’re trying to protect—”

“You want to use children as bait?”

“No.” Trista’s voice cut through, sharp and clear and very cold. She was looking at Varesa with an expression that had moved past dislike into something more specific and more dangerous. “No. That is not happening.”

“She’s right,” Vael said.

The room went quiet. Trista’s eyes cut to him — surprised, maybe, that he’d sided with her. Then he continued.

The room sat with this.

Varesa looked at him with the expression of someone being offered something they find genuinely unnecessary.

“No,” she said.

Trista exhaled in relief so profound it was nearly audible.

“Then it’s decided.” Ragnik stood. His voice carried the weight of a man who had made difficult calls before and was prepared to make them again. “Rebecca — start getting the shelter ready. We need it cleared and stocked.” He looked at the dead warrior on the floor, and his face did something brief and private. “I need three volunteers. This man came here to warn us. He deserves to go into the ground properly before we go out to meet what killed him.”

Three warriors stepped forward without hesitation. Ragnik nodded his thanks and said nothing else about it.

“I’ll get the riders organized,” Trista said, already moving toward the stairs. “And the volunteers for the long path. I want them briefed properly — they choose with full information.”

The room began to move. The chaos of minutes ago had become something different — directed, purposeful, still frightened but wearing the fear differently. Working with it rather than under it.

Vael stood at the table a moment longer, looking at the map. The two paths to the chokepoint. The ridgelines above. The ten minutes of open ground between the tavern and the place where they would make their stand.

He felt a presence beside him and looked up.

Varesa was looking at the map too. Up close, she was more precisely unsettling — the coat that absorbed light rather than reflecting it, the bow across her back strung with something that moved very slightly even in still air, as though it existed in a small persistent wind. Her eyes, when they flicked to his, were the grey of a sky about to do something definitive.

“The hand sign you used,” she said quietly. “Against the Framedious.”

He said nothing.

“I saw it from the treeline.” She held his gaze for a moment, then looked back at the map. “You have more than one.”

She moved away before he could respond, folding back into the organized movement of the room, and was somehow already less visible than her position should have allowed.

Vael was sitting on the porch steps when the morning found its full voice.

Around him the world was deceptively quiet — white ground, pale sky, the bare treeline standing in its long rows of dead and half-dead. Cold air that tasted of frost and something underneath it, some mineral edge he was starting to recognize.

The feeling in his chest was the feeling of standing at the top of something very high, just before the decision to jump.

He heard the door open and close behind him as people moved in and out, final preparations, last conversations. Somewhere inside Figo was running through weapon checks with two of the stronger warriors, his voice steady and instructive. James was likely drinking something and knowing something and saying nothing about either. Trista was upstairs, calm and deliberate, explaining to volunteers what they were volunteering for.

Varesa was wherever Varesa was, which was to say she was invisible until she wasn’t.

The sound reached him before he could name what it was — not loud, not close, but there. From the horizon, northeast, the direction the map had shown and that his blood had already been pointing toward without asking his opinion.

A roar. Low enough that it moved through the ground as much as the air. The sound of something very large and very awake and coming from somewhere that used to be far away and wasn’t, quite, anymore.

Vael was on his feet.

“IT’S ALMOST TIME!”

He turned. The door was already opening — people spilling out onto the porch and into the yard, drawn by the sound, and by his voice, and by the thing those two things together meant.

“GET EVERYONE READY! MOVE! NOW!”

Behind him, in the organized chaos of the tavern’s final preparations, four figures were already in motion — Figo strapping Sheela into place with practiced hands, jaw set; Trista descending the stairs with the calm of someone who has made peace with the next hour; James tucking his flask away with a small, private salute to no one; and Varesa, moving through the room like smoke given direction, already outside before anyone saw her leave.

The sun was up.

The horizon was not, quite, still.

Part 3 — The Hunt

The preparations had the particular quality of things done carefully by people who understand they may not get a second chance.

Vael moved through them slowly, taking stock.

The carts were being loaded with quiet efficiency — blankets, water, what little food could be spared, the volunteers lifting themselves up onto the boards with the deliberate movements of people choosing not to think too hard about what they were choosing. Husbands held wives for a beat longer than usual. A father knelt in the mud to be at eye level with his son, said something Vael couldn’t hear, pressed his forehead to the boy’s. The boy nodded like he understood. He was maybe seven.

The short man climbed into the second cart without ceremony, adjusted his brass monocle, and immediately produced a small leather-bound book from somewhere in his robes and began reading. As though this were a carriage ride to somewhere mildly interesting.

Across the yard, Figo sat on an upturned log with Sheela across his knees, drawing a whetstone along her edge in long, even strokes. The sound was rhythmic, patient. His shoulders were set. His jaw was set. Every line of him said I am not afraid in the specific way that only people who are afraid ever need to say it. He paused once, glanced sideways toward the treeline, and Vael followed the look.

Varesa stood at the tree line’s edge — not in it, not quite out of it, occupying the threshold the way she seemed to occupy most spaces, as though full commitment to any one location was a vulnerability she’d long since stopped accepting. Her head was tilted slightly downward. Her eyes were on the ground fifty yards out. Then a hundred. Then further, tracking something through the frost and dead grass that no one else in the yard was positioned to see.

“Aren’t Dracol land creatures?” one of the warriors asked. The man with the broken jaw, arms folded, eyes on the same horizon Varesa was reading.

“Yes,” James said, from his position leaning against the tavern wall beside the entrance, arms folded, eyes half-closed. “But life is full of surprises.”

He said it lightly. His eyes were not light. They were fixed on a point above the horizon, and they were very wide, and in them was the expression of a man watching a probability he had calculated suddenly become real and finding that calculation had not fully prepared him for the reality of it.

“It’s here,” he said.

He pointed up.

The Dracol came out of the low grey clouds the way a nightmare comes — not all at once, but in pieces, each piece worse than the last. First the silhouette, vast and wrong against the pale sky, too large for anything that size to move that fast. Then the detail: obsidian skin that caught no light, that seemed to pull the grey from the clouds around it and keep it. Veins of lava ran through the body in cracked, glowing lines, mapping the creature’s skeleton in molten orange from the outside, as though its insides were trying to escape. Its eyes were pure lava — not glowing like an ember but liquid, roiling, each one a contained eruption barely held in the socket. They bulged slightly, strained, the eyes of something in permanent anguish that had decided to share that condition with everything it encountered.

It banked once, lazily, over the treeline, and the downdraft from wings it should not have had flattened the dead grass in a wide ring below it.

Figo had stopped sharpening Sheela.

His hands were still on the whetstone. His knuckles were pale. The shivering he’d been suppressing since the Framedious fight — since before that, since the name Dracol had first been spoken in the tavern — had returned and was no longer entirely suppressable.

Trista stepped up beside him. She didn’t say anything. She took his shaking hand in both of hers and the healing light came through, quieter than the battlefield version, warmer — the kind of magic that doesn’t fix a wound but steadies the person carrying it.

“We have a plan this time,” she said. “We won’t let it fail.”

Figo exhaled slowly through his nose. The shivering slowed. He looked at Sheela, then up at the thing circling their sky, then back at Sheela.

“No,” he agreed. “We won’t.”

The broken-jaw warrior took his horse at a run, swinging up without using the stirrup, the broadsword already shifted on his back for riding. The stocky man with the hand axes checked the straps on both weapons before mounting, methodical even now. The ink-fingered mage climbed more carefully, her staff strapped across the saddlebags, her free hand already moving through the preliminary gestures of whatever she was preparing to throw. The words of the chant were old — older than the guild, older than most of what stood around them. Where the four voices intersected at the shelter door, the air shimmered faintly, the way air shimmers above hot stone in summer. Something about the quality of the light around it changed very slightly, the way a room changes when a window is covered. The mage withdrew his hand and looked at his three colleagues. Something unspoken passed between them.

They had twenty-five minutes.

If that, said the look on the lean mage’s face, to no one. If that.

“No turning back,” he said, to the assembled riders, to Sheela, to the cold morning air. “It’s show time.”

Ragnik stood at the tavern door. He had seen things, in his years — had the face of a man who had, and who intended to stand in this doorway and watch these people leave with full awareness of what some of them might not come back to. He looked at Vael last.

Vael gave him a nod. Ragnik returned it.

“Good luck,” Ragnik said. “All of you.”

He stepped back inside. The door closed.

Twenty-five minutes.

Not hands — a gentling of the force, a redirection, the falling converting itself from fast to present in the last ten feet, until he arrived at the ground at a speed that was embarrassing rather than fatal and sat down in the frost with an ungraceful ahh that he hoped nobody had heard.

“Apologies, young man.” The voice came from a few feet away. Small, dry, unbothered. “I don’t always stick the landing when catching others. Bit of an imprecise application of the discipline.”

Atver stood nearby, his book closed now, one hand still raised, the brass monocle catching the morning light. He looked at Vael the way someone looks at a mildly interesting phenomenon they’ve added to a running list of mildly interesting phenomena.

“Thanks,” Vael said, from the ground. “I’ll manage.”

“Atver,” the small elf said, and offered a slight bow that was both formal and entirely relaxed.

“Vael,” Vael said, and stood.

Then he heard the screaming from the direction of the carts, and he ran.

Trista was already there.

So were three other healers, and they were not enough. The second cart had taken the deflected lava ball on its right flank — the wood there had gone to nothing, the canvas above it still burning at the edges in a low, orange crawl that the cold was slowly winning against. People were helping people out of the wreckage, some moving under their own power, some not.

Vael helped where he could. He moved between people, following the healers’ direction, doing what hands could do when magic wasn’t available. Lifting, steadying, carrying.

He found Trista in the center of it, crouched over a man who was not moving, both hands full of healing light, the light very white and very bright and working very hard. Her face told him what the light was telling her.

She stood after a moment.

“Five,” she said.

The word sat in the morning air.

“We lost five.”

She didn’t cry. She looked like she wanted to. She looked like she had made a decision about that, and the decision was not yet, and the cost of the decision was visible in the set of her jaw and the stillness of her hands and the way she didn’t look at anything directly for a few seconds.

The hunters around them were quiet. Not the silence of shock — the silence of people who have been in enough hard situations to know that some silences need to be sat with before they can be spoken about.

They came back to the tavern in the late morning light, which had turned thin and pale and said nothing about what the day had been.

Ragnik was at the door before they were through the gate.

His face opened when he saw them coming — relief, the particular relief of a man who had spent an hour in a sealed shelter listening to things he couldn’t see and had not known, until this moment, which of the faces he was watching for would appear. He counted them. He saw who was there.

He saw who wasn’t.

His face did not close exactly. But something in it changed — settled into a different shape, heavier, the shape of a man adding something to a weight he already carried and making room for it.

He held the door.

He didn’t say anything. There wasn’t anything that needed saying that the open door didn’t already say.

Rebecca appeared behind him, and her eyes went immediately to the same count Ragnik’s had — who was there, who was missing, what the faces of the people who were there were telling her about the people who weren’t. Her hand went to her mouth briefly.

Then she straightened.

“Come in,” she said. “All of you. Come inside.”

Vael was last through the door. He stopped in the threshold and looked back at the northeast — the direction the Dracol had gone, the direction the sound of it had faded into, the direction the fight had ended but the thing hadn’t.

Atver appeared beside him, smaller than Vael’s shoulder, book back in hand, monocle glinting.

“It will rest,” the small elf said, conversationally. “Regeneration at that level is metabolically expensive. It will find somewhere dark and deep and it will sleep, and when it wakes it will resume.”

“You know where,” Vael said. It wasn’t a question.

“I know the region,” Atver said carefully. “I know the kind of place it would choose. And I have a lead — on why it is the way it is. Why it’s enraged, why it broke its cycle, why it is this and not what it should be.” He paused. “Developing that lead took me a considerable amount of time.”

“How much time?”

“Roughly two hundred and forty years,” Atver said, without particular emphasis. “On and off.”

Vael looked at him for a moment.

“You should come inside,” he said.

“Yes,” Atver agreed pleasantly. “I believe I should.”

Vael stepped inside.

The door closed behind them.

The northeast horizon was quiet, and empty, and was not going to stay that way.

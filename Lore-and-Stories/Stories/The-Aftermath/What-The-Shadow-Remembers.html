Chapter 3: What the Shadow Remembers

Part 1 — The Framedious

The cart rolled back toward the guild at a comfortable pace, the horses finding their own rhythm on the familiar road. The afternoon light had gone thin and pale, filtering through the bare treeline in long, cold shafts.

Figo had been quiet for a while. For Figo, that never lasted.

“Hey.” He turned from the road, fixing V with the evaluating squint of a man who had spent decades reading fighters. “Your movement back there. Against that Dagaruma that came from hiding.” He gave a slow, approving nod. “It was natural. Instinctive. You’re a hunter, lad. Whatever else you’ve forgotten, that part of you remembers.”

V opened his mouth, closed it, then looked down at his hands. “It just… happened. My body moved before I thought about it.”

“That’s exactly what it means,” Figo said, turning back to the road with the satisfied air of a man whose point had been proven.

“See?” James hadn’t moved from his half-reclined position, arms folded behind his head, a smug grin stretching slowly across his face. “Told you all. My gut. Never. Wrong.” A private gleam crossed his eyes — there and gone in an instant. Right on time, he thought. Almost. He cleared his throat with great ceremony. “I believe this calls for a drink. A reward for outstanding instinct.”

Trista turned to look at him with an expression so flat it could have been used as a table. “A drink,” she repeated. “Is not a reward, James.”

“It is if you’re me.”

V laughed — a real one, surprised out of him. “I feel… clearer,” he said, and he meant it. Something had shifted in the fog behind his eyes. Not a memory, not yet, but a shape where one might eventually be. “Like something’s starting to come back.”

“PROGRESS,” Figo announced, at a volume entirely unsuited to the size of the cart.

Then he stopped.

His head turned — slowly, the way a veteran’s head turns when something in the air changes. His eyes went to the west, to the dark line of trees beyond the ridge, and he said nothing for exactly three seconds.

The screech came from that direction.

It wasn’t loud in the way thunder is loud. It was loud in the way that made the ears feel wrong, like the sound was happening slightly inside the skull rather than outside it. The horses screamed. The yoke harness — old wood, dried out by weeks of cold — took the frequency like a hammer blow. A crack ran across the beam. Then another. Then the whole assembly splintered apart in a single sharp report, and the horses were gone, traces snapping behind them as they tore into the treeline and disappeared.

The cart, suddenly without anything pulling it, lurched hard to the left, tipped, and deposited all four of them onto the frost-hardened road.

V hit the ground and rolled, coming up onto one knee with his ears ringing. He looked up.

“…Framedious,” James said quietly. He was already on his feet, which was either impressive or suspicious. His face — for the first time since V had known him — had shifted. Not afraid. Not alarmed. Just… present. Alert in a way that sat strangely on features usually arranged into theatrical laziness.

“Time to lock in,” he added, glancing at V with a small smile. Your turn, kid. Right on schedule.

The Framedious stood at the tree line.

Seven feet tall at the shoulder — not counting the fifth limb, which was something between a leg and a spear, terminating in a rigid, glistening point that scraped the ground as the creature shifted its weight. It moved on the other four limbs with a horrible, fluid efficiency, and its body was armored in overlapping plates the color of old bone.

“Where’s its face?” V said.

“Look up,” Figo said, very quietly.

The Framedious answered the question by rearing back and leaping — a single, explosive movement that seemed to defy the weight of the thing — and as it rose, it showed its underside. The face was there, in the center of its belly, surrounded by the roots of all five limbs. A wide, wet, lipless mouth, ringed with teeth that curved inward.

“Fun fact,” James said pleasantly, “they eat and poop from the same place.”

“James,” said Trista, Figo, and V, in perfect unison.

The creature landed hard enough to split the frozen mud, and Trista was already moving. Her hands came up, fingers spreading wide, and she threw — not a weapon, exactly, but a compressed sphere of what looked like luminous powder that detonated against the Framedious’s chest in a cascading shower of iridescent light. The creature’s limbs seized. It staggered sideways, slowed, its movements suddenly laboring through an invisible resistance.

“Go!” Trista shouted.

Figo went.

Sheela came off his back in a single practiced arc, and he closed the distance at that startling, armor-defying speed, swinging into the Framedious’s flank with everything he had. The blade connected. The creature took one step back — actually moved, actually yielded — and then, while Figo was still in the long recovery of the swing, the fifth limb came around in a fast, short arc and opened a deep gash across his right shoulder.

Figo didn’t make a sound. He set his jaw and repositioned.

Trista threw the second bomb larger this time, and the burst staggered the Framedious backward far enough to buy a breath. She sprinted to Figo’s side, both hands already glowing, and spoke the chant low and fast beneath her breath.

“May our wounds curse our enemies.”

The light moved from her hands into Figo’s shoulder — and then, impossibly, the Framedious flinched. Its equivalent limb twitched and pulled inward, the same gash opening across its plating in a mirror of the wound it had just dealt.

“Woah,” V breathed.

The Framedious noticed. It screamed — that same skull-filling frequency — and charged.

It wasn’t slowed this time. It hit Figo full on, all the weight of it behind the impact, and sent him skidding back into the treeline. He struck a pine hard enough to crack the lower trunk, bark exploding outward, and dropped to one knee. He drove Sheela into the ground to stop himself falling further.

“Figo!” Trista was already running toward him. “The fifth limb — it’s venomous! He’s been hit, he needs—” She wheeled toward James. “James!”

James was lying with his back against the overturned cart, V’s pack arranged under his head like a pillow. His eyes were closed. His chest rose and fell with the slow, even rhythm of a man getting excellent sleep.

Trista made a sound that was not quite a word.

V watched Figo plant Sheela in the frozen ground and use the handle to haul himself upright, knuckles white, jaw locked. The big man’s right arm was hanging wrong. The shoulder wound was deep, and whatever was in the Framedious’s fifth limb had already started — there was a grey tinge spreading under the skin at the collar, slow and deliberate.

He’s still trying to fight, V realized. He’s going to try to fight it like that.

V’s feet were moving before he’d decided anything. He stepped past Trista, past the radius of relative safety she’d carved with her glitter bombs, and put himself between Figo and the Framedious, which had stopped its charge and was now tilting its belly-face toward him with a kind of cold, assessing curiosity.

“V.” Figo’s voice, behind him. Low. “You don’t have to do this.”

“I know.”

What am I doing? V thought, staring at the thing’s wet, inward-curving teeth, at the fifth limb dragging a pale groove through the mud as the creature shifted toward him. What in the actual hell am I doing right now? It has a poisoned leg-spear. It ate Figo’s shoulder. I don’t even know what I am.

The Framedious tilted its weight forward.

I am going to die in this road and I still don’t even know my name.

“I can’t keep being the backpack boy,” he said, mostly to himself.

Figo made a sound behind him — half protest, half something that might, in another context, have been pride. He heard the scrape of Sheela being lifted again, the old warrior refusing to stay down, using her as a third leg to push himself upright.

The Framedious charged.

All five limbs moved this time, the full terrible coordination of the thing unleashed at once, the belly-mouth screaming, poisoned saliva stringing from the fifth limb’s point in long, glittering threads. Trista screamed his name. Her hands were up, another bomb forming between her palms, but it wasn’t ready, it wasn’t going to be ready—

V’s right hand moved.

He didn’t decide to move it. It moved the way his feet had moved against the Dagaruma, the way his body had dodged before his mind caught up — pure muscle memory from a life he couldn’t see. His fingers folded inward, two knuckles raised, the shape his hand made casting a long, specific shadow on the ground before him even in the flat grey afternoon light.

The shadow rippled.

Something answered from beneath it.

A claw came first — enormous, dark as the space between stars, made of something that was not quite solid and not quite light. Then the shape of a wolf assembled itself from V’s shadow, rising without sound, without any of the drama the moment deserved. It simply was there, the way a thing is there when it has always been waiting.

It moved once.

The strike was surgical. A single sweep of one claw across the Framedious’s charging bulk — and the creature came apart. Not slowly. Not with a fight. The wound the shadow left didn’t bleed so much as open, and what had been a seven-foot apex predator folded into the mud with a sound like the world exhaling.

Silence.

The shadow wolf was gone. V’s hand was at his side. He was staring at the space where the Framedious had been, his own breathing loud in his ears, his fingers faintly tingling.

“…Well,” Figo said, from somewhere behind him. His voice was very quiet. “I’ll be damned.”

Trista hadn’t moved. The bomb she’d been building had dissipated between her palms, forgotten. She was looking at V — then at the shadow — then at the place the wolf had been — then, very slowly, at the cart where James was still apparently asleep.

Something connected behind her eyes.

Her expression went through several phases in quick succession, arriving finally at a particular kind of fury that was entirely too calm to be safe.

“Damn you, James,” she said, softly. “You could have just told us, you absolute rat.”

From behind the cart came the long, peaceful sound of someone snoring.

V turned around. He looked at his hand. He looked at Figo, who was still leaning on Sheela, grey-faced and bleeding but watching him with an expression that had moved well past surprise into something slower and more complicated.

“I don’t…” V started. Stopped. “I don’t know what that was.”

“I do,” Trista said. She was still staring at James. “Or at least — I know what it means.” She finally looked at V, and the fury in her face had made room for something else. Something almost like awe. “But that conversation can wait. Right now I need to stop Figo from dying.”

Figo, for his part, had the decency to look mildly embarrassed about the dying situation.

“I’m fine,” he said.

“You have venom in your shoulder.”

“I’ve had worse.”

“When?”

He paused. “…Give me a moment, I’ll think of something.”

V looked between the two of them, then at the wreckage of the cart, then at the Framedious, then at his own hand one more time.

Somewhere in the fog of his memory, something had shifted. Not a face. Not a place. Just a word — rising from somewhere deeper than memory, from whatever part of him had just moved his hand without asking permission.

A name.

Vael.

He held it in his mind for a moment, testing it, the way you test ice before putting your weight on it. It held. It felt like his.

“Hey,” he said quietly, interrupting nothing in particular.

Trista looked up from where she was already kneeling beside Figo, hands glowing.

“My name,” V said. “I think… I think it’s Vael.”

A beat of silence.

“FINALLY!” James shouted from behind the cart, in the wide-awake voice of a man who had not been asleep at all. “I WAS WONDERING WHEN YOU’D FIGURE THAT OUT!”

Trista closed her eyes.

“I’m going to kill him,” she said, with great serenity, “after I finish healing Figo.”

Part 2 — The Tapilca

James’s response to Trista’s threat arrived with the unruffled calm of a man who had already seen how this conversation ended.

“You knew that I knew that you knew that this outcome is entirely to our benefit, Trista.” He said it the way someone reads a sentence they’ve already memorized, then pivoted without drawing breath. “Okay! Here is the plan—”

Nobody noticed Figo quietly losing the battle to stay upright. His grip on Sheela loosened by degrees, the great axe serving its second purpose of the day as a leaning post, and then even that wasn’t enough.

“I’ll just…” he murmured, to no one in particular. “…rest for a while.”

He went down slowly, which was somehow more alarming than if he’d simply collapsed.

“Tapilcas!” James announced, spinning on his heel with the energy of a man presenting a solution he’d had in his pocket the entire time. “That’ll get him back to new. Vael — with me. Trista, stay here, keep him breathing. We won’t be long.” He turned and offered her a wink of such specific confidence that it stopped just short of infuriating.

Trista looked at Figo’s grey face. Then at James. Then at the middle distance, communing silently with whatever reserve of patience she had left.

“Fine,” she said. “Don’t take long. And Vael—” her eyes moved to him, sharp and serious, “—we’re talking about your powers when you get back.”

“Understood.” Vael grabbed James by the sleeve and pulled him toward the treeline. “Let’s go.”

“This way!” James pointed confidently in the wrong direction.

Vael looked at the direction. Looked at James. Turned them both around and walked the correct way without comment.

Thirty minutes into the forest, the world grew quiet in a way that felt deliberate.

It was the kind of quiet that took a moment to identify — not peaceful, exactly, but emptied. The Starfall had been months ago and the forest had not forgotten. Half the trees they moved through were dead on their feet, bark split and grey, branches bare not from winter but from something more permanent. The stardust had settled into the soil and the roots had drawn it up like poison through a straw. Some trees had fallen already, their trunks collapsed inward, soft and blackened. Others still stood, holding the shape of living things while being nothing of the sort.

What birds remained moved in small, nervous clusters rather than the easy scatter of a healthy wood. No undergrowth rustled. No distant calls answered each other across the canopy. The forest existed, but quietly, and carefully, as though it understood it was operating on reduced numbers and didn’t want to draw attention to the fact.

Vael walked through it and felt the absence like a pressure behind the eyes.

“Figo’s going to be fine,” he said, mostly to fill the silence. “Right?”

“Mm.” James had his hands in his pockets, moving through the dead wood with the relaxed familiarity of someone on an evening stroll. “Probably.”

“Probably.”

“Relax. Trista’s bought him time. We just need to move.” He glanced sideways. “Speaking of which — congratulations, by the way. On the name.”

Vael blinked. In the chaos of the Framedious and Figo going down, he’d almost forgotten the moment had happened. Vael. It still felt like pressing a bruise — tender, but real. “Thanks. I think.”

“We’ll celebrate properly when we’re back.” James said it with the warm finality of someone marking something off a list. “Now. Tapilcas. You want to know what you’re looking for?”

“That would help, yes.”

James tilted his head back, apparently organizing information he found mildly tedious to explain. “Right, the memory. Okay. Tapilcas grow from a specific tree — you’ll know the tree when you see it, it’s pink. Entirely pink, bark included, because the flowers cover the whole thing. The flowers themselves are called tapils — small, blue and pink, they grow in clusters all over the branches. Pretty enough.” He waved a hand. “But what you want is a tapilca. That’s a tapil that’s had a full summer of sun and good nutrition. It grows larger than the others and it starts producing a honey-like fluid from its center — you’ll see it dripping if you look closely. That fluid is what we need. Antivenom for most of what lives in this region, Framedious included.”

Vael turned this over. “And if they’re rare — how do we find one without searching the entire forest?”

“Hah.” James grabbed his arm and pulled him sideways into a low bush in one smooth motion, crouching without any apparent urgency. “Easy. We cheat.” He pointed through the branches. “Look.”

Vael looked. He scanned the undergrowth for something large, something dangerous, something proportionate to the problem they were trying to solve—

And then he found it. Barely twenty feet away, half-hidden in the shadow of a collapsed trunk: a creature no larger than his fist, round and absurdly fluffy, with a tail that seemed to account for half its total volume. Two enormous cheeks bulged on either side of its face. Green stripes ran along its back in uneven lines, the color close enough to the dead moss that Vael had looked directly at it twice without seeing it.

It was, objectively, very cute.

Aww, Vael thought, and immediately felt strange about thinking it given the circumstances.

“Davoura,” James murmured. “They live for Tapil trees. Follow it, it leads us right to one. Simple.”

“Simple,” Vael agreed, watching the small creature stuff something into its cheeks with dedicated efficiency. “Let’s go.”

Following a Davoura through a winter forest turned out to require a specific skill set that neither of them had formally trained for.

The creature moved in short, decisive bursts — several feet, then stillness, then several more feet in a direction that seemed to bear no relation to the previous one. It paused to investigate things. It doubled back. At one point it sat entirely motionless for ninety seconds for no discernible reason before abruptly sprinting sideways into a thicket.

James and Vael followed it through all of this in a low, shuffling crouch that was doing neither of them any favors, communicating in gestures that grew increasingly frantic as the Davoura began, unmistakably, to sense something was wrong.

It stopped. Sat up straight. Its enormous eyes swept the treeline behind it.

“That cheeky little thing,” James murmured.

“What?” Vael breathed.

“It’s stressed. It feels us.” James didn’t move a muscle. “If we spook it here we lose the trail entirely.”

The Davoura sat a moment longer, cheeks bulging, tail flicking in slow arcs. Then it bolted — straight up the nearest tree trunk, claws finding purchase in the dead bark, and vanished into the upper branches.

“We lost it—”

“We’re already here,” James said, and walked calmly around the other side of the tree.

Vael followed, and stopped.

The Tapil tree stood in a small natural clearing where two fallen trunks had opened a gap in the canopy. In the pale winter light filtering through, it glowed. There was no other word for it — the pink of the bark, the dense clusters of blue-and-pink tapils covering every branch from root to crown, the whole thing so saturated with color in a forest this grey and dead that it looked like something from a different world bleeding through. Several Davoura moved in its upper branches, unhurried and proprietary, stuffing tapils into their cheeks with professional focus.

“Davouras eat the tapils,” James said, coming to stand beside him. “And hunt for the tapilcas specifically. Territorial about them too.” He looked satisfied. “Now find one that’s dripping. Larger than the others, honey coming from the center.”

Vael moved around the base of the tree, scanning the lower branches, crouching to check the clusters within reach. The tapils were beautiful up close — petals the deep blue of a midnight sky with pink edges, clustered so thickly that the branches beneath were invisible. He moved along one low branch, checking each flower, and then—

There. Bigger than its neighbors by half, the petals slightly separated, a slow golden bead forming at its center and running down the outside of the petal in a thin, gleaming line.

Vael reached up carefully and closed his fingers around the stem.

Something landed on his hand.

The Davoura that had felt followed — their original quarry, cheeks still full — sat on his knuckles, looked him directly in the eye, bit the tapilca stem clean through, and was gone up the tree trunk before Vael had processed what had happened.

He stared at his empty hand.

“Hey!!” He was moving before the indignation had finished arriving, sprinting after the creature to the base of the tree, craning upward as the Davoura disappeared into the canopy. He stood there for a moment, breathing hard, one hand on the bark, the small creature nowhere to be seen.

“This is going to be impossible,” he said.

“Patience,” said James, from somewhere behind him. A short pause. “And — there we are.” The sound of a stem being carefully separated from a branch. “Got one. Let’s head back.”

Vael turned to find James holding the tapilca with the careful, two-fingered grip of someone handling something fragile, the honey already beading at its center, golden and slow. He looked entirely unbothered. He also looked, Vael noticed, just slightly more focused on moving quickly than his usual performance of ease would suggest.

They turned back toward the road.

The forest was quieter on the return walk, or maybe Vael was just more aware of it now. The dead trees stood in rows on either side, patient and still. Somewhere above them a single bird called twice and went silent.

The question had been sitting in Vael’s chest since the fight. He let it out.

“James.” He kept his eyes on the path. “When we were fighting the Framedious. You knew, didn’t you. You knew I’d step up. You knew what would happen.” A beat. “When you looked at me — right before the charge. You knew.”

James walked a few steps without answering. Then:

“I don’t know you,” he said. “Not personally. No.” He looked up at the canopy, at the dead branches crosshatched against the pale sky. He took a slow breath, the kind that comes before something true. “I have a curse. Others might call it a gift, I suppose, but I’ve had it longer than them, so I get to name it.” He paused. “I can see probabilities. When I look at someone — really look — I see the likelihood of outcomes. Good ones. Bad ones. Every shape a moment could take and how likely each one is to happen.”

Vael processed this quietly. “So you saw a probability that I would step up. That my power would emerge.” He thought about it. “It was a gamble. You just… took your chances with Figo’s life.”

“Yup!” James said, and did a small, inexplicable celebratory shuffle without breaking stride.

Vael stared at him. “That’s insane.”

“Probably.” The shuffle stopped. James was quiet for a moment, and something in his posture shifted — barely perceptible, but there, like a door opened a crack onto a darker room. “Most of the time… the probabilities I see are bad. Awful, sometimes. I see outcomes people don’t know are coming and can’t be warned about. I see the weight of what’s likely, and what’s likely is often—” He stopped. Reached into his coat. Produced a small, battered flask of ale and took a long pull from it. Exhaled. “This helps. Takes the edge off the numbers.”

Vael said nothing. There didn’t seem to be anything useful to say.

“But you, Vael.” James looked at him sideways, and for a moment — just a moment — the lazy theatrical mask was entirely gone, and what was underneath it was something older and more tired and completely sincere. “Your probabilities are interesting. I haven’t seen a spread like yours in a long time. Maybe ever.” The corner of his mouth pulled up. “I want to see how it all unfolds. That’s why I wanted you on the team. That’s why I’ll see it through.”

Vael turned this over in the silence of the dead wood around them.

“And Trista and Figo,” he said finally. “They trust your judgment because of this. Because they know what you can see.”

“Bingo.” James tucked the flask away. Hiccupped, once, with dignity. “Lesh go,” he announced, switching registers without warning. “Shave Figo!!”

They found Trista exactly where they’d left her, kneeling beside Figo in the grey afternoon light, both hands pressed to his chest, the healing glow coming from her fingers dimmer now than it had been — the particular dimness of something running on fumes. Her shoulders were tight. Her jaw was set. She looked up when she heard them coming and the expression on her face did about four things in quick succession, ending somewhere between relief and the specific fury of someone who has been holding everything together alone and has feelings about that.

James held up the tapilca.

The death stare she leveled at him was magnificent. It communicated, without words, a detailed record of every grievance she had accumulated since meeting him, organized chronologically and cross-referenced by category.

“Feed him,” she said. “Vael. You do it.”

“Me?”

She was already moving back to give him room, and Figo’s condition answered the question of whether there was time to discuss it. Vael knelt, took the tapilca carefully in both hands, and tilted the flower over Figo’s parted lips. The honey came slowly at first — a single gold thread — then steadier, catching the last of the afternoon light as it fell.

They waited.

The grey at Figo’s collar began, very slowly, to recede. The tight, shallow rhythm of his breathing deepened by degrees. Color returned to his face in the gradual, stubborn way that color returns to things that aren’t ready to give up.

Trista made a sound that was not quite a word — something between a sigh and a prayer — and her hands finally went still. Then, without ceremony or warning, she sat down where she was standing and did not get up.

Vael moved fast enough to catch her before she hit the ground, lowering her gently into the frost-stiff grass.

She was already asleep before she was fully horizontal.

He sat back on his heels and looked at the three of them — Figo breathing slow and even, Trista folded into the exhausted sleep of someone who had simply run out of self — and felt something settle in his chest. Not peace, exactly. Something quieter than that.

“James,” he said, standing. “Watch duty.”

“Aww—”

“Watch. Duty.”

James looked at him for a moment, then at the treeline, then produced his flask again with a philosophical shrug. “Fine.”

Vael collected what dry wood he could find within a short radius — harder work than it should have been, the starfall having robbed the forest of most of its good tinder — and built the fire low and practical, the kind that lasts rather than the kind that impresses. He set the camp with quiet efficiency, moving between his sleeping teammates with the ease of someone doing something their body understood even when their mind was still catching up.

When he finally straightened and turned to gather the last of the wood, he stopped.

The treeline was dark. The fire behind him threw his shadow long across the frost, and beyond its reach the forest was simply black — the compressed black of a winter night with no moon yet and too many dead trees to let in what stars there were.

But between two of those trees — thirty feet out, maybe forty, right at the edge of where the firelight gave up — there was a shape.

Still. Upright. Watching.

Not an animal. The proportions were wrong for that, too deliberate in their stillness, too vertical. A figure. He could make out almost nothing — a silhouette only, and within the silhouette a quality of shadow that seemed somehow denser than the dark around it, as though the darkness there had been gathered rather than simply fallen.

He blinked.

The space between the trees was empty.

No sound. No movement. Nothing to suggest anything had ever been there — except that the frost on the ground between those two trees was undisturbed, and something about the shape of the dark there still felt occupied in a way the rest of the treeline didn’t.

Vael stood very still for a long moment, firewood in his arms, heart doing something careful and measured in his chest.

Then James spoke from behind him, entirely casual.

“Better get that fire built, Vael. It’s going to be a cold one.”

Vael looked at him. James was looking at the flask in his hands, turning it slowly, his expression unreadable.

He knew. Of course he knew.

Vael turned back to the treeline one more time. Empty. Just trees.

He added the wood to the fire and said nothing.
